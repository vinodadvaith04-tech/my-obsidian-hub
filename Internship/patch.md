Patch Clamp Electrophysiology — Interview Prep Guide A ground-up walkthrough of the concepts, numbers, and vocabulary that come up in patch-clamp interviews. Organized so you can skim section headers the night before and drill into whichever part feels shaky. --- ## 1. The big picture: what patch clamp actually measures A glass micropipette forms a tight seal onto a tiny patch of cell membrane, electrically isolating that patch (or the whole cell) so you can either: - **Voltage clamp**: hold the membrane potential fixed at a value you set, and measure the *current* needed to do that (this current equals the current flowing through open channels). - **Current clamp**: inject a *current* you set, and measure the resulting *voltage* (this is how you record action potentials and see how a cell responds to synaptic-like inputs). The core trick that makes it "patch" clamp rather than old-school sharp-electrode recording is the **gigaseal**: a seal resistance in the gigaohm range between pipette and membrane, which makes the noise low enough to resolve currents from a handful of channels, or even a single channel. --- ## 2. The five recording configurations All configurations start from the same first step — a gigaseal in **cell-attached** mode — and branch from there. | Configuration | How you get there | What you see | Typical use | |---|---|---|---| | **Cell-attached** | Seal onto intact membrane, don't rupture | Single-channel currents from a resident patch, in the channel's native lipid/ionic environment; cytoplasm undisturbed | Studying channels without dialyzing the cell; measuring "true" resting activity | | **Whole-cell** | From cell-attached, rupture the patch (suction or "zap") | Sum of currents across the entire cell membrane; pipette solution dialyzes the cytoplasm | The workhorse mode: PSCs, action potentials, I-V curves, most standard neuroscience recordings | | **Inside-out excised** | From cell-attached, pull the pipette away; a patch of membrane comes off, resealing with the *cytoplasmic* face now exposed to the bath | Single-channel currents while you manipulate the intracellular side (perfuse the bath with second messengers, ATP, etc.) | Testing intracellular modulators of channel gating | | **Outside-out excised** | From whole-cell, pull the pipette away; membrane reseals with the *extracellular* face exposed to the bath | Single/few-channel currents while you control and rapidly change the extracellular side | Testing receptor pharmacology, fast agonist application, receptor kinetics | | **Perforated patch** | Cell-attached seal, but the internal solution contains a pore-forming antibiotic (nystatin, amphotericin-B, or gramicidin) that perforates the patch instead of rupturing it | Whole-cell-like electrical access, but small pores only pass monovalent ions — large intracellular molecules (second messengers, proteins) stay intact | Preserving intracellular signaling; avoiding "washout" of the very thing you're studying (e.g., second-messenger cascades, native Cl⁻ gradients) | **Trade-off worth stating out loud in an interview:** conventional whole-cell gives you low access resistance and full control of intracellular ionic composition, but it *dialyzes the cell* — you lose diffusible intracellular components over minutes, which can run down currents or change baseline properties ("washout"). Perforated patch avoids this at the cost of higher noise, higher and less stable access resistance, and losing single-channel resolution. --- ## 3. Voltage clamp vs. current clamp — the amplifier's two jobs - **Voltage clamp**: a feedback amplifier compares your commanded voltage to the actual membrane voltage and injects whatever current is needed to eliminate the difference. That injected current *is* your signal — it reflects ionic current flowing through open channels. This is how you measure PSCs, I-V relationships, and channel kinetics. - **Current clamp**: you inject a defined current (or none — "I=0" mode records the resting membrane potential passively) and read out the resulting voltage. This is how you record action potentials, resting membrane potential, input-output firing curves, and rheobase. A subtlety examiners like to probe: in voltage clamp, you're only "clamped" well near the pipette tip — more on **space clamp** below. --- ## 4. Rig components (know the vocabulary even if you won't build one) - **Amplifier + headstage**: the headstage sits right next to the pipette holder to minimize noise pickup before the tiny currents get amplified. - **Micromanipulator**: sub-micron precision positioning of the pipette. - **Faraday cage**: shields the rig from external electrical noise (this is why patch rigs are enclosed in metal mesh boxes). - **Vibration isolation table**: mechanical noise directly translates into seal instability at this spatial scale. - **Pressure control system**: syringe or electronic pressure controller to apply positive pressure (keeps the tip clean while approaching) and negative pressure/suction (for sealing and breaking in). - **Perfusion system**: continuously exchanges the bath (external) solution, often oxygenated ACSF bubbled with carbogen (95% O₂/5% CO₂) for brain slice work. - **Digitizer + acquisition software** (e.g., pClamp/Clampex, or open-source equivalents): converts the analog signal to digital and applies your voltage/current command protocols. --- ## 5. Pipettes - Pulled from **borosilicate** (or sometimes quartz, for very low-noise work) glass capillaries on a pipette puller, then often **fire-polished** at the tip. - Typical **pipette (open-tip) resistance**: roughly **2–10 MΩ** in physiological saline, with whole-cell somatic recordings commonly targeting **3–6 MΩ**. Lower resistance = bigger tip opening = easier whole-cell access but harder to get a good seal and more likely to damage the cell; higher resistance = better for small/fragile cells or single-channel work but higher series resistance once you're in. - Pipettes are often coated with **Sylgard** near the tip to reduce stray capacitance and pipette wall noise. - The pipette holds an **Ag/AgCl wire** in contact with the internal solution — this is your recording electrode; a matching Ag/AgCl reference/ground sits in the bath (sometimes via a salt-bridge/agar bridge to avoid contaminating the bath with pipette-filling ions). --- ## 6. Internal vs. external solution — and *why* they differ **External (bath/extracellular) solution** mimics the extracellular fluid — commonly an artificial cerebrospinal fluid (**ACSF**) with NaCl, KCl, CaCl₂, MgCl₂, NaHCO₃, glucose, sometimes HEPES, bubbled with carbogen if using bicarbonate buffering. **Internal (pipette/intracellular) solution** mimics the cytoplasm and, in whole-cell mode, *replaces* it (since the pipette dialyzes the cell). This is where the real design decisions live: - **K-gluconate–based internals** are the default for recording naturalistic membrane potentials and currents, because they preserve near-physiological K⁺-driven excitability. Used heavily for current-clamp/action-potential work and for isolating currents where you want K⁺ channels intact. - **Cs⁺-based internals** (Cs-methanesulfonate, Cs-gluconate, CsCl) replace K⁺ with Cs⁺, which blocks most K⁺ channels — this "flattens" the cell's own voltage-dependent conductances so you get a cleaner voltage clamp, essential when you're voltage-clamping synaptic currents over a wide range of holding potentials (e.g., mapping an NMDAR I-V curve out to +40/+60 mV). - **QX-314** (a lidocaine derivative) is frequently added to the internal solution specifically to block postsynaptic voltage-gated Na⁺ channels *from the inside*, preventing the cell from firing action potentials during voltage-clamp recordings — useful because APs escaping the clamp would contaminate your synaptic current measurements. - **CsCl vs. Cs-gluconate/Cs-methanesulfonate — the E/I balance decision:** this is very relevant to your own project. High **Cl⁻** internal solutions (CsCl) push the chloride reversal potential to near 0 mV, so GABA_A currents become large *inward* currents at negative holding potentials — convenient for isolating IPSCs from EPSCs by holding potential alone. Low-Cl⁻, gluconate/methanesulfonate-based internals keep E_Cl near physiological, hyperpolarized values, so GABA_A currents stay outward/small at typical holding potentials — better if you want to preserve the *native* driving force for Cl⁻ (which matters a lot if E/I balance and Cl⁻ gradients are literally your research question). - **Standard internal solution additives and why:** - **EGTA or BAPTA** — Ca²⁺ chelators/buffers, control intracellular free Ca²⁺ (BAPTA is faster-binding, used when you need to buffer Ca²⁺ very close to a channel or synapse). - **Mg-ATP, Na-GTP** — energy substrates to keep the cell's ATP/GTP-dependent processes (including many channels and pumps) functional after dialysis. - **HEPES** — pH buffer. - **Phosphocreatine** (sometimes with creatine phosphokinase) — helps maintain ATP levels during long recordings. - **Osmolarity matching**: internal solution is typically kept slightly **hypo-osmotic relative to the external** (e.g., internal ~280–290 mOsm vs external ~300–310 mOsm) — a small osmotic gradient helps maintain seal stability and cell health; if internal is too hyperosmotic the cell can shrink/swell in ways that destabilize the seal or damage the cell. - **pH**: both solutions are typically adjusted to ~7.2–7.4 (internal with KOH/CsOH, external with NaOH, or via bicarbonate/CO₂ buffering for ACSF). --- ## 7. Getting the seal: pressures, seal test, and the gigaseal Step-by-step (the "classic" manual sequence — automated rigs follow the same logic): 1. **Fill the pipette**, mount it, and apply **light positive pressure** (roughly a few mmHg, sometimes called "light continuous positive pressure") before it touches the bath and while approaching the cell. This keeps the tip clean and prevents debris or bath solution from clogging or contaminating it, and you should see the open-tip resistance stay stable as you approach. 2. **Zero the pipette offset** once the tip is in the bath, and apply a small **seal test** pulse (a repeating test pulse, often on the order of 5–10 mV) so you can watch the current response on-screen — a square-wave current response tells you the tip is open and unobstructed. 3. **Approach the target cell** under visual guidance, watching the resistance readout. Contact with the cell membrane causes a small resistance increase ("dimpling" the membrane is a classic visual sign in slice work). 4. **Release the positive pressure**, and often apply **gentle negative pressure (suction)** — commonly in the range of roughly **−20 to −100 mmHg**, applied briefly and gently rather than as one hard pull. Resistance should climb rapidly from the initial ~tens of MΩ into the **gigaohm range (commonly defined as ≥1 GΩ, ideally several GΩ)** — this is the **gigaseal**. 5. Once sealed, **switch the holding potential** toward the cell's expected resting potential (often around −60 to −70 mV) before doing anything further — this reduces the size of any leak current if the seal isn't perfect and protects the membrane patch. 6. **Compensate fast capacitance** (cancels the pipette's own capacitive transient) at this stage, while still cell-attached. 7. **Break in** to go whole-cell: either a stronger, brief suction pulse, or an electrical **"zap"** (a brief high-voltage pulse) that ruptures the patch without disrupting the surrounding gigaseal. You'll see the test-pulse response suddenly develop large **capacitive transients** — this is your visual confirmation that you're now whole-cell, since the amplifier is now charging/discharging the whole cell's membrane capacitance, not just the tiny patch. **Why the gigaseal matters, mechanistically**: a lower-resistance seal means an alternative, leaky current path around the pipette tip. That leak current adds noise and, worse, actively distorts your measured currents (some of your test current escapes through the seal rather than through channels). Gigaohm-range seals push that leak current down to essentially negligible levels. --- ## 8. Core electrical concepts — the ones interviewers love to probe with "explain it from scratch" ### Driving force **Driving force = V_m − E_rev** (membrane potential minus the reversal potential for the ion/channel in question). It's the electrochemical "push" on ions through an open channel at a given moment. Current only flows if driving force is nonzero; the direction of current flow (inward vs outward) flips sign when V_m crosses E_rev. ### Reversal potential (E_rev) The membrane potential at which net current through a given channel is zero — where the electrical and chemical gradients for that ion (or that channel's mix of permeant ions) exactly balance. For a channel selective to one ion, E_rev equals that ion's **equilibrium potential**, calculable via the **Nernst equation**: **E_ion = (RT/zF) · ln([ion]_out / [ion]_in)** For channels permeable to multiple ions (e.g., non-selective cation channels, or channels with mixed Na⁺/K⁺ permeability), the reversal potential is a weighted combination of each permeant ion's equilibrium potential and relative permeability — captured by the **Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) equation**. Know the conceptual difference: Nernst gives you a single ion's equilibrium potential; GHK gives you the *membrane's* resting potential (or a channel's reversal potential) when several ions with different permeabilities are involved. ### Ohm's law, applied to channels **I = g · (V_m − E_rev)**, where g is conductance. This is the single most-used equation in patch clamp — it's literally "current = conductance × driving force." Rearranged, you can extract conductance from measured current and known/assumed driving force, which is how conductance-voltage relationships and I-V curves get built. ### Equilibrium potential vs. reversal potential — a common trick question For a single-ion-selective channel, they're the same number. But "equilibrium potential" strictly refers to a single ion's Nernst potential (a chemistry concept — the balance point for that species), while "reversal potential" refers to the *observed* zero-current point of a specific current/channel, which may reflect a mix of ions. Being precise about this distinction reads well in an interview. --- ## 9. Resistances you need to know cold | Resistance | What it is | Typical value | Why it matters | |---|---|---|---| | **Pipette (open-tip) resistance** | Resistance of the pipette tip in bath solution before sealing | ~2–10 MΩ (often 3–6 MΩ for whole-cell somatic work) | Sets your starting point; trades off seal quality vs. ease of whole-cell access | | **Seal resistance** | Resistance between pipette and membrane after seal formation | Gigaseal = commonly ≥1 GΩ, ideally several GΩ | Determines leak current and noise; a "bad seal" (hundreds of MΩ) badly contaminates your signal | | **Access/series resistance (R_a or R_s)** | Resistance of the pathway from pipette electrode into the cell interior once whole-cell (through the ruptured patch hole) | Commonly **2–20 MΩ** in healthy whole-cell recordings; many labs set a hard cutoff (e.g., reject or exclude if R_s exceeds roughly 20–30 MΩ, or if it changes by more than ~20–30% during the recording) | This is your single biggest source of voltage-clamp error — see below. Monitored continuously throughout a recording, not just at break-in. | | **Input/membrane resistance (R_m or R_in)** | Resistance of the cell's own membrane (reflects how many channels are open at rest) | Roughly 100s of MΩ up to a few GΩ depending on cell type/size | Reflects cell health and excitability; a sudden large drop often signals a problem (e.g., a "leaky"/dying cell) | ### Series resistance error — the concept to really nail Because R_s sits in series between your amplifier's command voltage and the actual cell membrane, some of your commanded voltage is "used up" across R_s rather than reaching the membrane, especially when currents are large (**V_error = I × R_s**, basically Ohm's law again). This means: - Bigger currents → bigger voltage errors → your true clamp potential deviates from what you commanded. - Most amplifiers offer **series resistance compensation** (a feedback circuit that corrects for a fraction of this error in real time). In practice, labs commonly compensate somewhere in the 60–80% range; pushing compensation too high risks oscillation/instability in the clamp circuit. - Even after compensation, some **residual/effective R_s** remains, and its error can be estimated afterward via Ohm's law, though real (measured) errors are sometimes smaller than the naive Ohm's-law estimate suggests, particularly for fast/inactivating currents — worth knowing as a nuance if pressed. - Practical takeaway you can say out loud: "I monitor R_s throughout the recording and exclude cells where it exceeds our cutoff or drifts substantially, because increasing R_s during a recording will make currents look smaller and kinetics look slower even if nothing biological changed." ### Space clamp A related but distinct concept: even with R_s handled, you can only really "clamp" the voltage well close to the pipette (e.g., the soma). Distal dendrites, especially in cells with complex, extensive dendritic arbors, are electrotonically far from the pipette and are **imperfectly clamped** — their true local voltage lags/differs from your command potential. This matters especially for interpreting synaptic currents generated far out on dendrites, and for interactions between excitatory and inhibitory conductances that are electrotonically separated from the soma. --- ## 10. Capacitance and time constants - **Membrane capacitance (C_m)**: the cell membrane acts as a capacitor (lipid bilayer separating charge). Bigger cells have bigger C_m. When you break into whole-cell, the sudden appearance of a **large capacitive transient** in response to your test pulse is the tell-tale sign you've achieved whole-cell access (you're now charging the whole cell's membrane, not just a tiny patch). - **Capacitance compensation**: amplifiers offer "fast" capacitance compensation (cancels pipette-and-holder capacitance, done in cell-attached mode before break-in) and "slow"/whole-cell capacitance compensation (cancels the cell's own membrane capacitance, done after break-in). This isn't just cosmetic — uncompensated capacitive transients can obscure fast synaptic currents and interfere with series resistance estimation. - **Membrane time constant (τ_m = R_m × C_m)**: how quickly the membrane potential responds to a current step; relevant to temporal summation of synaptic inputs and to how "leaky" vs. "integrative" a neuron behaves. - **Pipette time constant (τ_p = R_s × C_p)**: relevant to how fast your clamp can respond — this is part of why very high R_s limits your recording bandwidth for fast events. --- ## 11. Liquid junction potential (LJP) — the one people forget When your internal (pipette) solution and external (bath) solution have different ionic compositions, a small voltage forms at the interface where they meet, because different ions (e.g., small, fast Cl⁻ vs. large, slow gluconate) diffuse across that junction at different rates. This creates a **standing offset** that's baked into your "zeroed" pipette potential before you ever seal onto a cell. - Practically: when you zero the pipette offset in the bath, you're actually zeroing out (V_bath − V_pipette_offset), and the LJP means that zero point isn't the *true* zero — so every subsequent voltage you read (including your holding potential) is systematically off by the LJP. - Typical magnitude: commonly on the order of a few mV up to roughly 10–15 mV for common gluconate/methanesulfonate-based internals, though this depends heavily on the specific solutions and can be measured empirically (comparing pipette potential in bath solution vs. in internal solution) or calculated with software. - **Correction convention**: whether you add or subtract depends on your sign convention and which solution is which — this trips people up, so if asked, describe the *logic* (measure LJP by comparing pipette-in-bath vs. pipette-in-internal-in-bath, and understand which direction it shifts your apparent V_m) rather than trying to recite a memorized sign. - Many papers report their LJP was calculated but *not* corrected (or *was* corrected) — always worth checking/stating explicitly in methods, and a common interview probe is simply "do you correct for LJP, and why or why not." --- ## 12. Key protocols you should be able to describe ### I-V (current-voltage) protocol A family of voltage steps (e.g., stepping from a holding potential to a range of test potentials, often in ~10 mV increments) while measuring the resulting current at each step. Plotting current vs. command voltage reveals a channel's voltage-dependence — where it activates, its reversal potential, and its conductance. ### Activation / inactivation protocols Voltage-gated channels (especially Na⁺ and K⁺ channels) are characterized with paired-pulse protocols: a **conditioning/pre-pulse** at varying potentials followed by a fixed **test pulse**, used to build steady-state activation and inactivation curves (fraction of channels available vs. voltage), often fit with Boltzmann functions. ### mEPSC / mIPSC recording (miniature postsynaptic currents) — directly relevant to your project Miniature currents reflect **spontaneous, action-potential-independent** vesicle release (one vesicle at a time), so the pharmacology strips away everything except that: - **TTX** (blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels) — removes action potentials, so you only see spontaneous, non-network-driven release (this is what makes them "miniature" rather than just "spontaneous," sPSCs). - To isolate **mEPSCs** (excitatory, AMPAR/NMDAR-mediated): block GABA_A receptors with **bicuculline** or **picrotoxin**; often also record near resting/negative holding potentials (commonly around −60 to −70 mV) where NMDAR contribution is minimal due to Mg²⁺ block, so what you see is largely AMPAR-mediated. - To isolate **mIPSCs** (inhibitory, GABA_A-mediated): block AMPA/kainate receptors with **CNQX** (or **NBQX**) and NMDARs with **APV** (**D-AP5**); if the internal solution has high Cl⁻ (e.g., CsCl-based), mIPSCs appear as *inward* currents even at negative holding potentials, which is a common design choice specifically so you can record mIPSCs without having to depolarize the cell. - A useful design point for your own project: your **choice of holding potential relative to E_Cl** determines whether GABAergic currents appear inward or outward, and by how much — this directly ties to the internal-solution driving-force discussion in Section 6, and is exactly the kind of question ("why did you hold at that potential, and why that internal solution?") an interviewer will ask about a proposal on E/I balance. - Analysis afterward is usually automated event detection (template matching or amplitude-threshold-based), yielding frequency (≈ related to presynaptic release probability/number of release sites) and amplitude (≈ related to postsynaptic receptor number/sensitivity) as the two headline readouts — a classic, useful heuristic even though both can be influenced by both sides of the synapse. ### Evoked PSCs and paired-pulse ratio (PPR) Instead of spontaneous minis, you electrically or optogenetically stimulate a presynaptic pathway and record the evoked response. Delivering **two closely-spaced stimuli** and comparing the second response's amplitude to the first (paired-pulse ratio) is a classic, if imperfect, readout of presynaptic release probability: **facilitation** (2nd > 1st) suggests low initial release probability, **depression** (2nd < 1st) suggests high initial release probability. ### Current-clamp / firing protocols A series of current steps (increasing amplitude) while recording membrane potential, used to build **input-output (F-I) curves** (firing frequency vs. injected current), and to determine **rheobase** (minimum current to elicit a spike) and **action potential threshold**. --- ## 13. Troubleshooting / artifacts interviewers may ask about - **Rising access resistance during a recording**: often means the whole-cell opening is resealing or getting clogged with debris — signal amplitude will artifactually shrink and kinetics will artifactually slow, mimicking a "real" biological change if you're not careful. This is exactly why R_s is monitored throughout, not just at the start. - **Leak current**: a non-zero holding current at rest often reflects an imperfect seal or an unhealthy cell; many analyses do **leak subtraction** (e.g., a P/N protocol: scaling down small sub-threshold voltage steps to estimate and subtract the linear/leak component from the real, non-linear voltage-gated response). - **Washout**: in whole-cell mode, diffusible intracellular components dialyze out over the first several minutes, which is why many labs wait for a stabilization period, and why perforated patch is chosen when washout would confound the biology being studied. - **Poor space clamp**: distorts synaptic current kinetics/amplitude for inputs far from the soma (see Section 9) — relevant if you're comparing proximal vs. distal inputs, or interactions between excitatory and inhibitory conductances at different dendritic locations. - **Seal breakdown / cell death mid-recording**: watch for sudden loss of gigaseal, dramatic changes in input resistance, or resting potential drifting toward 0 mV. - **Osmotic/pressure mismatches**: too much positive pressure during approach can blow cells away or damage tissue; too much suction during sealing can rupture the cell prematurely instead of forming a seal. --- ## 15.
